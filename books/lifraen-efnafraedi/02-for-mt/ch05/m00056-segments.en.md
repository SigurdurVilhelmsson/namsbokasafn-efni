Meso Compounds

<!-- SEG:m00056:para:para-00001 -->
Let’s look at another example () of a compound with more than one chirality center: the tartaric acid used by Pasteur. The four stereoisomers can be drawn as follows:

<!-- SEG:m00056:alt:fig-00001-alt -->
The mirror image of 2 R, 3 R tartaric acid is 2 S, 3 S and mirror image of 2 R, 3 S tartaric acid is 2 S, 3 R.

<!-- SEG:m00056:para:para-00002 -->
The 2[[i:R]],3[[i:R]] and 2[[i:S]],3[[i:S]] structures are nonsuperimposable mirror images and therefore represent a pair of enantiomers. A close look at the 2[[i:R]],3[[i:S]] and 2[[i:S]],3[[i:R]] structures, however, shows that they [[i:are]] superimposable, and thus identical, as can be seen by rotating one structure 180°.

<!-- SEG:m00056:alt:fig-00002-alt -->
2 R, 3 S tartaric acid is rotated at 180 degrees to form 2 S, 3 R tartaric acid. Both structures are identical.

<!-- SEG:m00056:para:para-00003 -->
The 2[[i:R]],3[[i:S]] and 2[[i:S]],3[[i:R]] structures are identical because the molecule has a plane of symmetry and is therefore achiral. The symmetry plane cuts through the C2–C3 bond, making one half of the molecule a mirror image of the other half ([[xref:fig-00003]]). Because of the plane of symmetry, the molecule is achiral, despite the fact that it has two chirality centers. Such compounds, which are achiral, yet contain chirality centers, are called [[term:meso compounds|term-00001]] [[b:(me-zo)]]. Thus, tartaric acid exists in three stereoisomeric forms: two enantiomers and one meso form.

<!-- SEG:m00056:caption:fig-00003-caption -->
[[b:A symmetry plane through the C2–C3 bond of [[i:meso]]-tartaric acid makes the molecule achiral.]]

<!-- SEG:m00056:alt:fig-00003-alt -->
The wedge-dash structure and ball-and-stick model of tartaric acid. C2 and C3 have a horizontal symmetry plane.

<!-- SEG:m00056:para:para-00004 -->
Some physical properties of the three stereoisomers are listed in [[xref:table-00001]]. The (+)- and (−)-tartaric acids have identical melting points, solubilities, and densities, but they differ in the sign of their rotation of plane-polarized light. The meso isomer, by contrast, is diastereomeric with the (+) and (−) forms. It has no mirror-image relationship to (+)- and (−)-tartaric acids, is a different compound altogether, and thus has different physical properties.

<!-- SEG:m00056:example-title:exam-00001-title -->
Distinguishing Chiral Compounds from Meso Compounds

<!-- SEG:m00056:para:para-00005 -->
Does [[i:cis]]-1,2-dimethylcyclobutane have any chirality centers? Is it chiral?

<!-- SEG:m00056:para-title:para-00006-title -->
Strategy

<!-- SEG:m00056:para:para-00006 -->
To see whether a chirality center is present, look for a carbon atom bonded to four different groups. To see whether the molecule is chiral, look for the presence or absence of a symmetry plane. Not all molecules with chirality centers are chiral overall&#8212;meso compounds are an exception.

<!-- SEG:m00056:para-title:para-00007-title -->
Solution

<!-- SEG:m00056:para:para-00007 -->
A look at the structure of [[i:cis]]-1,2-dimethylcyclobutane shows that both methyl-bearing ring carbons (C1 and C2) are chirality centers. Overall, though, the compound is achiral because there is a symmetry plane bisecting the ring between C1 and C2. Thus, the molecule is a meso compound.

<!-- SEG:m00056:alt:fig-00004-alt -->
The structure of cis-1,2-dimethylcyclobutane. The bond between C1 and C2 has a vertical symmetry plane.

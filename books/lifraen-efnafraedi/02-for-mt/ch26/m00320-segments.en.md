Synthesis of Amino Acids

<!-- SEG:m00320:para:para-00001 -->
[[i:α]]-Amino acids can be synthesized in the laboratory using some of the reactions discussed in previous chapters. One of the oldest methods of [[i:α]]-amino acid synthesis begins with [[i:α]] bromination of a carboxylic acid by treatment with Br[[sub:2]] and PBr[[sub:3]] (the Hell–Volhard–Zelinskii reaction; ). S[[sub:N]]2 substitution of the [[i:α]]-bromo acid with ammonia then yields an [[i:α]]-amino acid.

<!-- SEG:m00320:alt:fig-00001-alt -->
4-Methylpentanoic acid reacts with bromine, phosphorus tribromide, and water to form 2-bromo-4-methyl-pentanoic acid. This further reacts with excess ammonia to form (R, S)-leucine in 45 percent yield.

The Amidomalonate Synthesis

<!-- SEG:m00320:para:para-00003 -->
A more general method for preparation of [[i:α]]-amino acids is the [[i:amidomalonate synthesis,]] a straightforward extension of the malonic ester synthesis (). The reaction begins with the conversion of diethyl acetamidomalonate into an enolate ion by treatment with base, followed by S[[sub:N]]2 alkylation with a primary alkyl halide. Hydrolysis of both the amide protecting group and the esters occurs when the alkylated product is warmed with aqueous acid, and decarboxylation then takes place to yield an [[i:α]]-amino acid. Reductive Amination of [[i:α]]-Keto Acids

<!-- SEG:m00320:para:para-00005 -->
Yet another method for the synthesis of [[i:α]]-amino acids is by reductive amination of an [[i:α]]-keto acid with ammonia and a reducing agent. Alanine, for instance, is prepared by treatment of pyruvic acid with ammonia in the presence of NaBH[[sub:4]]. As described in , the reaction proceeds through formation of an intermediate imine which is then reduced.

<!-- SEG:m00320:alt:fig-00003-alt -->
Pyruvic acid reacts with ammonia and sodium borohydride to form an imine intermediate that further reacts to form (R, S)-alanine.

<!-- SEG:m00320:title:sect-00005-title -->
Enantioselective Synthesis

<!-- SEG:m00320:para:para-00006 -->
The synthesis of an [[i:α]]-amino acid from an achiral precursor by any of the methods just described yields a racemic mixture, with equal amounts of [[i:S]] and [[i:R]] enantiomers. To use an amino acid in the laboratory synthesis of a naturally occurring protein, however, the pure [[i:S]] enantiomer must be obtained.

<!-- SEG:m00320:para:para-00007 -->
Two methods are used in practice to obtain enantiomerically pure amino acids. One way requires resolving the racemic mixture into its pure enantiomers (). A more direct approach, however, is to use an enantioselective synthesis to prepare only the desired [[i:S]] enantiomer directly. As discussed in the [[docref:Chapter 19 [[i:Chemistry Matters]]|m00225#sect-00006]], the idea behind enantioselective synthesis is to find a chiral reaction catalyst that will temporarily hold a substrate molecule in an unsymmetrical, chiral environment. While in that chiral environment, the substrate may be more open to reaction on one side than on another, leading to an excess of one enantiomeric product.

<!-- SEG:m00320:para:para-00008 -->
William Knowles at the Monsanto Company discovered in 1968 that [[i:α]]-amino acids can be prepared enantioselectively by hydrogenation of a [[i:Z]] enamido acid with a chiral hydrogenation catalyst. ([[i:S]])-Phenylalanine, for instance, is prepared at 98.7% purity, contaminated by only 1.3% of the ([[i:R]]) enantiomer, when using a chiral rhodium catalyst. For this discovery, Knowles shared the 2001 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

<!-- SEG:m00320:alt:fig-00004-alt -->
A (Z) enamido acid reacts with hydrogen, rhodium catalyst, sodium hydroxide, and water to form (S)-phenylalanine.

<!-- SEG:m00320:para:para-00009 -->
The most effective catalysts for enantioselective amino acid synthesis are coordination complexes of rhodium(I) with 1,5-cyclooctadiene (COD) and a chiral diphosphine such as ([[i:R]],[[i:R]])-1,2-bis([[i:o]]-anisylphenylphosphino)ethane, the so-called DiPAMP ligand. This complex owes its chirality to the presence of trisubstituted phosphorus atoms ().

<!-- SEG:m00320:alt:fig-00005-alt -->
A rhodium coordination complex with boron tetrafluoride ion. The rhodium ligand is a benzene ring with a methoxy on C 1 and a methyl group on C 2 position.

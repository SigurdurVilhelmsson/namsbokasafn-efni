Describing a Reaction: Equilibria, Rates, and Energy Changes

<!-- SEG:m00083:para:para-00001 -->
Every chemical reaction can go in either a forward or reverse direction. Reactants can go forward to products, and products can revert to reactants. As you may remember from your general chemistry course, the position of the resulting chemical equilibrium is expressed by an equation in which [[i:K]][[sub:eq]], the equilibrium constant, is equal to the product concentrations multiplied together, divided by the reactant concentrations multiplied together, with each concentration raised to the power of its coefficient in the balanced equation. The value of the equilibrium constant tells which side of the reaction arrow is energetically favored. If [[i:K]][[sub:eq]] is much larger than 1, then the product concentration term [C][[i:[[sup:c]]]][D][[i:[[sup:d]]]] is much larger than the reactant concentration term [A][[i:[[sup:a]]]][B][[i:[[sup:b]]]], and the reaction proceeds as written from left to right. If [[i:K]][[sub:eq]] is near 1, appreciable amounts of both reactant and product are present at equilibrium. And if [[i:K]][[sub:eq]] is much smaller than 1, the reaction does not take place as written but instead goes in the reverse direction, from right to left.

<!-- SEG:m00083:para:para-00006 -->
What determines the magnitude of the equilibrium constant? For a reaction to have a favorable equilibrium constant and proceed as written, the energy of the products must be lower than the energy of the reactants. In other words, energy must be released. This situation is analogous to that of a rock poised precariously in a high-energy position near the top of a hill. When it rolls downhill, the rock releases energy until it reaches a more stable, low-energy position at the bottom.

<!-- SEG:m00083:para:para-00007 -->
The energy change that occurs during a chemical reaction is called the [[term:Gibbs free-energy change (Δ[[i:G]])|term-00001]], which is equal to the free energy of the products minus the free energy of the reactants: Δ[[i:G]] = [[i:G]][[sub:products]] − [[i:G]][[sub:reactants]]. For a favorable reaction, Δ[[i:G]] has a negative value, meaning that energy is lost by the chemical system and released [[i:to]] the surroundings, usually as heat. Such reactions are said to be [[term:exergonic|term-00002]]. For an unfavorable reaction, Δ[[i:G]] has a positive value, meaning that energy is absorbed by the chemical system [[i:from]] the surroundings. Such reactions are said to be [[term:endergonic|term-00003]].

<!-- SEG:m00083:para:para-00008 -->
You might also recall from general chemistry that the [[i:standard]] free-energy change for a reaction is denoted as Δ[[i:G]]°, where the superscript ° means that the reaction is carried out under standard conditions, with pure substances in their most stable form at 1 atm pressure and a specified temperature, usually 298 K. For biological reactions, the standard free-energy change is denoted as Δ[[i:G]]°′ and refers to a reaction carried out at pH = 7.0 with solute concentrations of 1.0 M.

<!-- SEG:m00083:para:para-00014 -->
The [[term:enthalpy change (Δ[[i:H]])|term-00004]], also called the [[term:heat of reaction|term-00005]], is a measure of the change in total bonding energy during a reaction. If Δ[[i:H]] is negative, as in the reaction of HBr with ethylene, the products have less energy than the reactants. Thus, the products are more stable and have stronger bonds than the reactants, heat is released, and the reaction is said to be [[term:exothermic|term-00006]]. If Δ[[i:H]] is positive, the products are less stable and have weaker bonds than the reactants, heat is absorbed, and the reaction is said to be [[term:endothermic|term-00007]]. For example, if a reaction breaks reactant bonds with a total strength of 380 kJ/mol and forms product bonds with a total strength of 400 kJ/mol, then Δ[[i:H]] for the reaction is 380 kJ/mol - 400 kJ/mol = −20 kJ/mol and the reaction is exothermic.

<!-- SEG:m00083:para:para-00019 -->
Knowing the value of [[i:K]][[sub:eq]] for a reaction is useful, but it’s important to realize its limitations. An equilibrium constant tells only the position of the equilibrium, or how much product is theoretically possible. It doesn’t tell the rate of reaction, or how fast the equilibrium is established. Some reactions are extremely slow even though they have favorable equilibrium constants. Gasoline is stable at room temperature, for instance, because the rate of its reaction with oxygen is slow at 298 K. Only at higher temperatures, such as contact with a lighted match, does gasoline react rapidly with oxygen and undergo complete conversion to the equilibrium products water and carbon dioxide. Rates (how fast a reaction occurs) and equilibria (how much a reaction occurs) are entirely different.

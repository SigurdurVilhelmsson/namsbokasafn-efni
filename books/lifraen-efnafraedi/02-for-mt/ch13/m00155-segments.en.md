<!-- SEG:m00155:para:para-00001 -->
Numerous techniques developed in recent years have made it possible to obtain enormous amounts of information from [[sup:13]]C NMR spectra. Among these techniques is one called [[term:[[i:DEPT–NMR]]|term-00001]], for [[i:distortionless enhancement by polarization transfer,]] which makes it possible to distinguish between signals due to CH[[sub:3]], CH[[sub:2]], CH, and quaternary carbons. That is, the number of hydrogens attached to each carbon in a molecule can be determined.

<!-- SEG:m00155:para:para-00002 -->
A DEPT experiment is usually done in three stages, as shown in [[xref:fig-00001]] for 6-methyl-5-hepten-2-ol. The first stage is to run an ordinary spectrum (called a broadband-decoupled spectrum) to locate the chemical shifts of all carbons. Next, a second spectrum called a DEPT-90 is run, using special conditions under which only signals due to CH carbons appear. Signals due to CH[[sub:3]], CH[[sub:2]], and quaternary carbons are absent. Finally, a third spectrum called a DEPT-135 is run, using conditions under which CH[[sub:3]] and CH resonances appear as positive signals, CH[[sub:2]] resonances appear as negative signals—that is, as peaks below the baseline—and quaternary carbons are again absent.

<!-- SEG:m00155:caption:fig-00001-caption -->
[[b:DEPT–NMR spectra for 6-methyl-5-hepten-2-ol.]] Part [[b:(a)]] is an ordinary broadband-decoupled spectrum, which shows signals for all eight carbons. Part [[b:(b)]] is a DEPT-90 spectrum, which shows only signals for the two CH carbons. Part [[b:(c)]] is a DEPT-135 spectrum, which shows positive signals for the two CH and three CH[[sub:3]] carbons and negative signals for the two CH[[sub:2]] carbons.

<!-- SEG:m00155:alt:fig-00001-alt -->
The figure shows broadband-decoupled, D E P T-90, and D E P T-135 spectra of 6-methyl-5-hepten-2-ol.

<!-- SEG:m00155:para:para-00003 -->
Putting together the information from all three spectra makes it possible to tell the number of hydrogens attached to each carbon. The CH carbons are identified in the DEPT-90 spectrum, the CH[[sub:2]] carbons are identified as negative peaks in the DEPT-135 spectrum, the CH[[sub:3]] carbons are identified by subtracting the CH peaks from the positive peaks in the DEPT-135 spectrum, and quaternary carbons are identified by subtracting all peaks in the DEPT-135 spectrum from the peaks in the broadband-decoupled spectrum.

<!-- SEG:m00155:alt:fig-00002-alt -->
Broadband decoupled (C, C H, methylene, methyl), D E P T-90 (C H), and D E P T-135 groups (methyl, C H-positive, methylene-negative) are labeled.

<!-- SEG:m00155:example-title:exam-00001-title -->
Assigning a Chemical Structure from a [[sup:13]]C NMR Spectrum

<!-- SEG:m00155:para:para-00004 -->
Propose a structure for an alcohol, C[[sub:4]]H[[sub:10]]O, that has the following [[sup:13]]C NMR spectral data:

<!-- SEG:m00155:para:para-00005 -->
Broadband decoupled [[sup:13]]C NMR: 19.0, 31.7, 69.5 [[i:δ]];

<!-- SEG:m00155:para:para-00006 -->
DEPT-90: 31.7 [[i:δ]];

<!-- SEG:m00155:para:para-00007 -->
DEPT-135: positive peak at 19.0 [[i:δ]], negative peak at 69.5 [[i:δ]].

<!-- SEG:m00155:para-title:para-00008-title -->
Strategy

<!-- SEG:m00155:para:para-00008 -->
As noted in , it usually helps with compounds of known formula but unknown structure to calculate the compound’s degree of unsaturation. In the present instance, a formula of C[[sub:4]]H[[sub:10]]O corresponds to a saturated, open-chain molecule.

<!-- SEG:m00155:para:para-00009 -->
To gain information from the [[sup:13]]C data, let’s begin by noting that the unknown alcohol has four carbon atoms, yet has only three NMR absorptions, which implies that two of the carbons must be equivalent. Looking at chemical shifts, two of the absorptions are in the typical alkane region (19.0 and 31.7 [[i:δ]]), while one is in the region of a carbon bonded to an electronegative atom (69.5 [[i:δ]])—oxygen in this instance. The DEPT-90 spectrum tells us that the alkyl carbon at 31.7 [[i:δ]] is tertiary (CH); the DEPT-135 spectrum tells us that the alkyl carbon at 19.0 [[i:δ]] is a methyl (CH[[sub:3]]) and that the carbon bonded to oxygen (69.5 [[i:δ]]) is secondary (CH[[sub:2]]). The two equivalent carbons are probably both methyls bonded to the same tertiary carbon, (CH[[sub:3]])[[sub:2]]CH–. We can now put the pieces together to propose a structure: 2-methyl-1-propanol.

<!-- SEG:m00155:para-title:para-00010-title -->
Solution

<!-- SEG:m00155:alt:fig-00003-alt -->
Structure of 2-methyl-1-propanol with shift values of 69.5 (C 1), 31.7 (C 2), and 19.0 (C 3 and methyl).

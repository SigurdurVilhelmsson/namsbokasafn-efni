One possibility is that the protons are chemically unrelated and thus nonequivalent. If so, the products formed on substitution of H by X would be different constitutional isomers. In butane, for instance, the –CH[[sub:3]] protons are different from the –CH[[sub:2]]– protons. They therefore give different products on substitution by X than the –CH[[sub:2]] protons and would likely show different NMR absorptions.

<!-- SEG:m00150:item:list-00001-item-2 -->
A second possibility is that the protons are chemically identical and thus electronically equivalent. If so, the same product would be formed regardless of which H is substituted by X. In butane, for instance, the six –CH[[sub:3]] hydrogens on C1 and C4 are identical, would give the identical structure on substitution by X, and would show an identical NMR absorption. Such protons are said to be [[term:homotopic|term-00001]].

<!-- SEG:m00150:item:list-00001-item-3 -->
The third possibility is a bit more subtle. Although they might at first seem homotopic, the two –CH[[sub:2]]– hydrogens on C2 in butane (and the two –CH[[sub:2]]– hydrogens on C3) are in fact not identical. Substitution by X of a hydrogen at C2 (or C3) would form a new chirality center, so different enantiomers () would result, depending on whether the pro-[[i:R]] or pro-[[i:S]] hydrogen had been substituted (). Such hydrogens, whose substitution by X would lead to different enantiomers, are said to be [[term:enantiotopic|term-00002]]. Enantiotopic hydrogens, even though not identical, are nevertheless electronically equivalent and thus have the same NMR absorption.

<!-- SEG:m00150:item:list-00001-item-4 -->
The fourth possibility arises in chiral molecules, such as ([[i:R]])-2-butanol. The two –CH[[sub:2]]– hydrogens at C3 are neither homotopic nor enantiotopic. Because substitution of a hydrogen at C3 would form a [[i:second]] chirality center, different diastereomers () would result, depending on whether the pro-[[i:R]] or pro-[[i:S]] hydrogen had been substituted. Such hydrogens, whose substitution by X leads to different diastereomers, are said to be [[term:diastereotopic|term-00003]]. Diastereotopic hydrogens are neither chemically nor electronically equivalent. They are completely different and would likely show different NMR absorptions.

<!-- SEG:m00150:para:para-00001 -->
Because each electronically distinct hydrogen in a molecule has its own unique absorption, one use of [[sup:1]]H NMR is to find out how many kinds of electronically nonequivalent hydrogens are present. In the [[sup:1]]H NMR spectrum of methyl acetate shown previously in [[docref:m00145#fig-00001]]a, for instance, there are two signals, corresponding to the two kinds of nonequivalent protons present, CH[[sub:3]]C═O protons and –OCH[[sub:3]] protons.

<!-- SEG:m00150:para:para-00002 -->
For relatively small molecules, a quick look at the structure is often enough to decide how many kinds of protons are present and thus how many NMR absorptions might appear. If in doubt, though, the equivalence or nonequivalence of two protons can be determined by comparing the structures that would be formed if each hydrogen were replaced by an X group. There are four possibilities.

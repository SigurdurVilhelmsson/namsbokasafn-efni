Drawing Electron-Dot and Line-Bond Structures

<!-- SEG:m00165:para:para-00001 -->
The bonds we’ve seen in methane and ethane are called [[i:single bonds]] because they result from the sharing of one electron pair between bonded atoms. It was recognized nearly 150 years ago, however, that carbon atoms can also form [[i:double bonds]] by sharing two electron pairs between atoms or [[i:triple bonds]] by sharing three electron pairs. Ethylene, for instance, has the structure [[MATH:1]] and contains a carbon–carbon double bond, while acetylene has the structure [[MATH:2]] and contains a carbon–carbon triple bond.

<!-- SEG:m00165:para:para-00002 -->
How are multiple bonds described by valence bond theory? When we discussed [[i:sp]][[sup:3]] hybrid orbitals in [[docref:Section 1.6|m00163]], we said that the four valence-shell atomic orbitals of carbon combine to form four equivalent [[i:sp]][[sup:3]] hybrids. Imagine instead that the 2[[i:s]] orbital combines with only [[i:two]] of the three available 2[[i:p]] orbitals. Three [[term:[[i:sp]][[sup:2]] hybrid orbitals|term-00001]] result, and one 2[[i:p]] orbital remains unchanged. Like [[i:sp]][[sup:3]] hybrids, [[i:sp]][[sup:2]] hybrid orbitals are unsymmetrical about the nucleus and are strongly oriented in a specific direction so they can form strong bonds. The three [[i:sp]][[sup:2]] orbitals lie in a plane at angles of 120° to one another, with the remaining [[i:p]] orbital perpendicular to the [[i:sp]][[sup:2]] plane, as shown in [[xref:fig-00001]].

<!-- SEG:m00165:para:para-00003 -->
When two carbons with [[i:sp]][[sup:2]] hybridization approach each other, they form a strong [[i:σ]] bond by [[i:sp]][[sup:2]]–[[i:sp]][[sup:2]] head-on overlap. At the same time, the unhybridized [[i:p]] orbitals interact by sideways overlap to form what is called a [[term:pi ([[i:π]]) bond|term-00002]]. The combination of an [[i:sp]][[sup:2]]–[[i:sp]][[sup:2]] [[i:σ]] bond and a 2[[i:p]]–2[[i:p π]] bond results in the sharing of four electrons and the formation of a carbon–carbon double bond ([[xref:fig-00002]]). Note that the electrons in the [[i:σ]] bond occupy the region centered between nuclei, while the electrons in the [[i:π]] bond occupy regions above and below a line drawn between nuclei.

<!-- SEG:m00165:para:para-00004 -->
To complete the structure of ethylene, four hydrogen atoms form σ bonds with the remaining four [[i:sp]][[sup:2]] orbitals. Ethylene thus has a planar structure, with H–C–H and H–C–C bond angles of approximately 120°. (The actual values are 117.4° for the H–C–H bond angle and 121.3° for the H–C–C bond angle.) Each C–H bond has a length of 108.7 pm and a strength of 464 kJ/mol (111 kcal/mol).

<!-- SEG:m00165:caption:fig-00002-caption -->
[[b:The structure of ethylene.]] One part of the double bond in ethylene results from [[i:σ]] (head-on) overlap of [[b:[[i:sp]][[sup:2]] hybrid orbitals]], and the other part results from [[i:π]] (sideways) overlap of unhybridized [[b:[[i:p]] orbitals (red/blue)]]. The [[i:π]] bond has regions of electron density above and below a line drawn between nuclei.

<!-- SEG:m00165:alt:fig-00002-alt -->

<!-- SEG:m00165:para:para-00005 -->
As you might expect, the carbon–carbon double bond in ethylene is both shorter and stronger than the single bond in ethane because it has four electrons bonding the nuclei together rather than two. Ethylene has a [[MATH:3]] bond length of 134 pm and a strength of 728 kJ/mol (174 kcal/mol) versus a C–C length of 153 pm and a strength of 377 kJ/mol for ethane. The carbon–carbon double bond is less than twice as strong as a single bond because the sideways overlap in the [[i:π]] part of the double bond is not as great as the head-on overlap in the [[i:σ]] part.

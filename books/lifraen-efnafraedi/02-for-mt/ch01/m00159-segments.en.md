Atomic Structure: Orbitals

<!-- SEG:m00159:para:para-00001 -->
How are the electrons distributed in an atom? You might recall from your general chemistry course that, according to the [[i:quantum mechanical model]], the behavior of a specific electron in an atom can be described by a mathematical expression called a [[i:wave equation]]—the same type of expression used to describe the motion of waves in a fluid. The solution to a wave equation is called a [[i:wave function]], or [[term:orbital|term-00001]], and is denoted by the lowercase Greek letter psi ([[i:ψ]]).

<!-- SEG:m00159:para:para-00002 -->
When the square of the wave function, [[i:ψ]][[sup:2]], is plotted in three-dimensional space, an orbital describes the volume of space around a nucleus that an electron is most likely to occupy. You might therefore think of an orbital as looking like a photograph of the electron taken at a slow shutter speed. In such a photo, the orbital would appear as a blurry cloud, indicating the region of space where the electron has been. This electron cloud doesn’t have a sharp boundary, but for practical purposes we can set the limits by saying that an orbital represents the space where an electron spends 90% to 95% of its time.

<!-- SEG:m00159:para:para-00003 -->
What do orbitals look like? There are four different kinds of orbitals, denoted [[i:s, p, d,]] and [[i:f,]] each with a different shape. Of the four, we’ll be concerned primarily with [[i:s]] and [[i:p]] orbitals because these are the most common in organic and biological chemistry. An [[i:s]] orbital has a spherical shape, with the nucleus at its center; a [[i:p]] orbital has a dumbbell shape with two parts, or [[i:lobes]]; and four of the five [[i:d]] orbitals have a cloverleaf shape with four lobes, as shown in [[xref:fig-00001]]. The fifth [[i:d]] orbital is shaped like an elongated dumbbell with a doughnut around its middle.

<!-- SEG:m00159:para:para-00004 -->
The orbitals in an atom are organized into different layers around the nucleus called [[term:electron shells|term-00002]], which are centered around the nucleus and have successively larger size and energy. Different shells contain different numbers and kinds of orbitals, and each orbital within a shell can be occupied by two electrons. The first shell contains only a single [[i:s]] orbital, denoted 1[[i:s,]] and thus holds only 2 electrons. The second shell contains one 2[[i:s]] orbital and three 2[[i:p]] orbitals and thus holds a total of 8 electrons. The third shell contains a 3[[i:s]] orbital, three 3[[i:p]] orbitals, and five 3[[i:d]] orbitals, for a total capacity of 18 electrons. These orbital groupings and their energy levels are shown in [[xref:fig-00002]].

<!-- SEG:m00159:para:para-00005 -->
The three different [[i:p]] orbitals within a given shell are oriented in space along mutually perpendicular directions, denoted [[i:p]][[sub:x]], [[i:p]][[sub:y]], and [[i:p]][[sub:z]]. As shown in [[xref:fig-00003]], the two lobes of each [[i:p]] orbital are separated by a region of zero electron density called a [[term:node|term-00003]]. Furthermore, the two orbital regions separated by the node have different algebraic signs, + and −, in the wave function, as represented by the different colors in [[xref:fig-00001]] and [[xref:fig-00003]]. As we’ll see in [[b:Section 1.11]], these algebraic signs for different orbital lobes have important consequences with respect to chemical bonding and chemical reactivity.

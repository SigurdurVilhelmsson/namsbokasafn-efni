Oxidation of Alkenes: Epoxidation and Hydroxylation

<!-- SEG:m00095:para:para-00001 -->
Like the word [[i:reduction]] used in the previous section for the addition of hydrogen to a double bond, the word [[i:oxidation]] has a slightly different meaning in organic chemistry than what you might have previously learned. In general chemistry, an oxidation is defined as the loss of one or more electrons by an atom. In organic chemistry, however, an [[term:oxidation|term-00001]] is a reaction that results in a loss of electron density for carbon, caused either by bond formation between carbon and a more electronegative atom—usually oxygen, nitrogen, or a halogen—or by bond-breaking between carbon and a less electronegative atom—usually hydrogen. Note that an [[i:oxidation]] often adds oxygen, while a [[i:reduction]] often adds hydrogen.

In the laboratory, alkenes are oxidized to give [[i:epoxides]] on treatment with a [[i:peroxyacid]], RCO[[sub:3]]H, such as [[i:meta]]-chloroperoxybenzoic acid. An [[term:epoxide|term-00002]], also called an [[term:oxirane|term-00003]], is a cyclic ether with an oxygen atom in a three-membered ring. For example:

<!-- SEG:m00095:alt:fig-00001-alt -->
A reaction shows the epoxidation of cycloheptene with meta-chloroperoxybenzoic acid in dichloromethane to form epoxycycloheptane and meta-chlorobenzoic acid

<!-- SEG:m00095:para:para-00004 -->
Peroxyacids transfer an oxygen atom to the alkene with syn stereochemistry—both C−O bonds form on the same face of the double bond—through a one-step mechanism without intermediates. The oxygen atom farthest from the carbonyl group is the one transferred.

<!-- SEG:m00095:alt:fig-00002-alt -->
A reaction mechanism shows alkene reacting with peroxyacid to form epoxide and acid.

<!-- SEG:m00095:para:para-00005 -->
Another method for the synthesis of epoxides involves the use of halohydrins, prepared by electrophilic addition of HO−X to alkenes (). When a halohydrin is treated with base, HX is eliminated and an epoxide is produced.

<!-- SEG:m00095:alt:fig-00003-alt -->
Cyclohexene reacts with chlorine in the presence of water to form trans-2-chloro-cyclohexanol, which reacts with aqueous sodium hydroxide to form 1,2-epoxycyclohexane (73 percent yield), water, and sodium chloride.

<!-- SEG:m00095:para:para-00006 -->
Epoxides undergo an acid-catalyzed ring-opening reaction with water (a hydrolysis) to give the corresponding 1,2-dialcohol, or [[i:diol]], also called a [[term:glycol|term-00004]]. Thus, the net result of the two-step alkene epoxidation/hydrolysis is [[term:hydroxylation|term-00005]]—the addition of an −OH group to each of the two double-bond carbons. In fact, approximately 204 million tons of ethylene glycol, HOCH[[sub:2]]CH[[sub:2]]OH, most of it used for automobile antifreeze, are produced worldwide each year by the epoxidation of ethylene and subsequent hydrolysis.

<!-- SEG:m00095:alt:fig-00004-alt -->
An alkene undergoes epoxidation to form an epoxide, which reacts with hydronium ion to form a 1,2-diol product.

<!-- SEG:m00095:para:para-00007 -->
Acid-catalyzed epoxide opening begins with protonation of the epoxide to increase its reactivity, followed by nucleophilic addition of water. This nucleophilic addition is analogous to the final step of alkene bromination, in which a cyclic bromonium ion is opened by a nucleophile (). That is, a [[i:trans]]-1,2-diol results when an epoxycycloalkane is opened by aqueous acid, just as a [[i:trans]]-1,2-dibromide results when a cycloalkene is brominated. We’ll look at epoxide chemistry in more detail in [[b:Section 18.6]].

<!-- SEG:m00095:alt:fig-00005-alt -->
A reaction mechanism shows 1,2-epoxycyclohexane reacting with hydronium ion and water to form trans-1,2-cyclohexanediol (86 percent yield). At the bottom, cyclohexene reacts with bromine to form trans-1,2-dibromocyclohexane.

<!-- SEG:m00095:para:para-00008 -->
Hydroxylation can also be carried out directly (without going through an intermediate epoxide) by treating an alkene with osmium tetroxide, OsO[[sub:4]]. The reaction occurs with syn stereochemistry and does not involve a carbocation intermediate. Instead, it takes place through an intermediate cyclic [[i:osmate]], which is formed in a single step by addition of OsO[[sub:4]] to the alkene. This cyclic osmate is then cleaved using aqueous sodium bisulfite, NaHSO[[sub:3]].

<!-- SEG:m00095:alt:fig-00006-alt -->
1,2-Dimethylcyclopentene reacts with osmium tetroxide in pyridine to form a cyclic osmate intermediate, which reacts with N a H S O 3 in water to form cis-1,2-dimethyl-1,2-cyclopentanediol (87 percent yield).

<!-- SEG:m00095:para:para-00009 -->
Because OsO[[sub:4]] is both very expensive and [[i:very]] toxic, the reaction is usually carried out using only a small, catalytic amount of OsO[[sub:4]] in the presence of a stoichiometric amount of a safe and inexpensive co-oxidant such as [[i:N]]-methylmorpholine [[i:N]]-oxide, abbreviated NMO. The initially formed osmate intermediate reacts rapidly with NMO to yield the product diol plus [[i:N]]-methylmorpholine and reoxidized OsO[[sub:4]], which reacts with more alkene in a catalytic cycle.

<!-- SEG:m00095:alt:fig-00007-alt -->
1-Phenyl-cyclohexene reacts with catalytic osmium tetroxide in the presence of acetone and water, followed by  N-methylmorpholine N-oxide reduction to form cis-1-phenyl-1,2-cyclohexanediol (93 percent yield), osmium tetroxide, and N-methyl-morpholine.

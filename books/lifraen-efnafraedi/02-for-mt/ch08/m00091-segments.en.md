Halohydrins from Alkenes: Addition of HO-X

<!-- SEG:m00091:para:para-00001 -->
Another example of an electrophilic addition is the reaction an alkene with either Br[[sub:2]] or Cl[[sub:2]] in the presence of water to yield a 1,2-halo alcohol, called a [[term:halohydrin|term-00001]].

<!-- SEG:m00091:alt:fig-00001-alt -->
A reaction shows an alkene reacts with dihalide in the presence of water to form a halohydrin and hydrogen halide.

<!-- SEG:m00091:para:para-00002 -->
We saw in the previous section that when Br[[sub:2]] reacts with an alkene, the cyclic bromonium ion intermediate reacts with the only nucleophile present, Br[[sup:−]] ion. If the reaction is carried out in the presence of an additional nucleophile, however, the intermediate bromonium ion can be intercepted by the added nucleophile and diverted to a different product. In the presence of a high concentration of water, for instance, water competes with Br[[sup:−]] ion as a nucleophile and reacts with the bromonium ion intermediate to yield a [[i:bromohydrin]]. The net effect is addition of HO−Br to the alkene by the pathway shown in [[xref:fig-00002]].

<!-- SEG:m00091:caption:fig-00002-caption -->
[[b:Bromohydrin formation by reaction of an alkene with Br[[sub:2]] in the presence of water.]] Water acts as a nucleophile in step 2 to react with the intermediate bromonium ion.

<!-- SEG:m00091:alt:fig-00002-alt -->
A 3-step reaction mechanism shows the formation of 3-bromo-2-butanol and hydronium ion from 2-butene and molecular bromine.

<!-- SEG:m00091:para:para-00003 -->
In practice, few alkenes are soluble in water, and bromohydrin formation is often carried out in a solvent such as aqueous dimethyl sulfoxide, CH[[sub:3]]SOCH[[sub:3]] (DMSO), using a reagent called [[i:N]]-bromosuccinimide (NBS) as a source of Br[[sub:2]]. NBS is a stable, easily handled compound that slowly decomposes in water to yield Br[[sub:2]] at a controlled rate. Bromine itself can also be used in the addition reaction, but it is more dangerous and more difficult to handle than NBS.

<!-- SEG:m00091:alt:fig-00003-alt -->
In a reaction, styrene reacts with N-bromosuccinimide in the presence of water and D M S O to form 2-bromo-1-phenylethanol with 70 percent yield.

<!-- SEG:m00091:para:para-00004 -->
Notice that the aromatic ring in the above example does not react with Br[[sub:2]], even though it appears to have three carbon–carbon double bonds. As we’ll see in Section 15.2, aromatic rings are a good deal more stable and less reactive than might be expected.

Names and Structures of Alkyl Halides

<!-- SEG:m00113:para:para-00001 -->
Although commonly called [[i:alkyl halides]], halogen-substituted alkanes are named systematically as [[i:haloalkanes]] (), treating the halogen as a substituent on a parent alkane chain. There are three steps:

<!-- SEG:m00113:para:para-00002 -->
[[b:STEP 1]][[BR]][[b:Find the longest chain, and name it as the parent.]] If a double or triple bond is present, the parent chain must contain it.

<!-- SEG:m00113:para:para-00003 -->
[[b:STEP 2]][[BR]][[b:Number the carbons of the parent chain beginning at the end nearer the first substituent, whether alkyl or halo.]] Assign each substituent a number according to its position on the chain.

<!-- SEG:m00113:alt:fig-00001-alt -->
The structures of 5-bromo-2,4-dimethylheptane and 2-bromo-4,5-dimethylheptane in which the carbon chain is numbered and halogen and methyl substituents are highlighted in red and green respectively.

<!-- SEG:m00113:para:para-00004 -->
If different halogens are present, number each one and list them in alphabetical order when writing the name.

<!-- SEG:m00113:alt:fig-00002-alt -->
The structure of 1-bromo-3-chloro-4-methylpentane in which the carbon chain is numbered and bromine, chlorine, and methyl substituents are highlighted in red, blue, and green respectively.

<!-- SEG:m00113:para:para-00005 -->
[[b:STEP 3]][[BR]][[b:If the parent chain can be properly numbered from either end by step 2, begin at the end nearer the substituent that has alphabetical precedence.]]

<!-- SEG:m00113:alt:fig-00003-alt -->
The structure of 2-bromo-5-methylhexane in which the carbon chain is numbered and halogen and methyl substituents are highlighted in red and green respectively.

<!-- SEG:m00113:para:para-00006 -->
In addition to their systematic names, many simple alkyl halides are also named by identifying first the alkyl group and then the halogen. For example, CH[[sub:3]]I can be called either iodomethane or methyl iodide. Such names are well entrenched in the chemical literature and in daily usage, but they won’t be used in this book.

<!-- SEG:m00113:alt:fig-00004-alt -->
The structures of iodomethane (or methyl iodide), 2-chloropropane (or isopropyl chloride), and bromocyclohexane (or cyclohexyl bromide) with halogens highlighted in red.

<!-- SEG:m00113:para:para-00007 -->
Halogens increase in size going down the periodic table, so the lengths of the corresponding carbon–halogen bonds increase accordingly ([[xref:table-00001]]). In addition, C−X bond strengths decrease going down the periodic table. As we’ve been doing thus far, we’ll continue using an X to represent any of the halogens F, Cl, Br, or I.

In our discussion of bond polarity in functional groups in , we noted that halogens are more electronegative than carbon. The C−X bond is therefore polar, with the carbon atom bearing a slight positive charge ([[i:δ]]+) and the halogen a slight negative charge ([[i:δ]]−). This polarity results in a dipole moment for all the halomethanes ([[xref:table-00001]]) and implies that the alkyl halide C−X carbon atom should behave as an electrophile in polar reactions. We’ll soon see that this is indeed the case.

Alkyne Acidity: Formation of Acetylide Anions

<!-- SEG:m00109:para:para-00001 -->
The most striking difference between alkenes and alkynes is that terminal alkynes are relatively acidic. When a terminal alkyne is treated with a strong base, such as sodium amide, Na[[sup:+]] [[sup:&#8722;]]NH[[sub:2]], the terminal hydrogen is removed and the corresponding [[term:acetylide anion|term-00001]] is formed.

<!-- SEG:m00109:alt:fig-00001-alt -->
The figure shows the mechanism where alkyne reacts with sodium amide to form an acetylide anion and the byproduct is ammonia.

<!-- SEG:m00109:para:para-00002 -->
According to the Brønsted–Lowry definition (), an acid is a substance that donates H[[sup:+]]. Although we usually think of oxyacids (H[[sub:2]]SO[[sub:4]], HNO[[sub:3]]) or halogen acids (HCl, HBr) in this context, any compound containing a hydrogen atom can be an acid under the right circumstances. By measuring dissociation constants of different acids and expressing the results as p[[i:K]][[sub:a]] values, an acidity order can be established. Recall from that a lower p[[i:K]][[sub:a]] corresponds to a stronger acid and a higher p[[i:K]][[sub:a]] corresponds to a weaker one.

<!-- SEG:m00109:para:para-00003 -->
Where do hydrocarbons lie on the acidity scale? As the data in [[xref:table-00001]] show, both methane (p[[i:K]][[sub:a]] ≈ 60) and ethylene (p[[i:K]][[sub:a]] = 44) are very weak acids and thus do not react with any of the common bases. Acetylene, however, has p[[i:K]][[sub:a]] = 25 and can be deprotonated by the conjugate base of any acid whose p[[i:K]][[sub:a]] is greater than 25. Amide ion (NH[[sub:2]][[sup:&#8722;]]), for example, the conjugate base of ammonia (p[[i:K]][[sub:a]] = 35), is often used to deprotonate terminal alkynes.

<!-- SEG:m00109:para:para-00004 -->
Why are terminal alkynes more acidic than alkenes or alkanes? In other words, why are acetylide anions more stable than vinylic or alkyl anions? The simplest explanation involves the hybridization of the negatively charged carbon atom. An acetylide anion has an [[i:sp]]-hybridized carbon, so the negative charge resides in an orbital that has 50% “[[i:s]] character.” A vinylic anion has a [[i:sp]][[sup:2]]-hybridized carbon with 33% [[i:s]] character, and an alkyl anion ([[i:sp]][[sup:3]]) has only 25% [[i:s]] character. Because [[i:s]] orbitals are nearer the positive nucleus and lower in energy than [[i:p]] orbitals, the negative charge is stabilized to a greater extent in an orbital with higher [[i:s]] character ([[xref:fig-00002]]).

<!-- SEG:m00109:caption:fig-00002-caption -->

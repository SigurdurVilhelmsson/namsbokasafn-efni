Cyclic Structures of Monosaccharides: Anomers

<!-- SEG:m00303:para:para-00001 -->
We said in that aldehydes and ketones undergo a rapid and reversible nucleophilic addition reaction with alcohols to form hemiacetals.

<!-- SEG:m00303:alt:fig-00001-alt -->
The reversible reaction of an aldehyde with an alcohol in the presence of an acid catalyst to form a hemiacetal via nucleophilic substitution.

<!-- SEG:m00303:para:para-00002 -->
If the carbonyl and the hydroxyl group are in the same molecule, an intramolecular nucleophilic addition can take place, leading to the formation of a cyclic hemiacetal. Five- and six-membered cyclic hemiacetals are relatively strain-free and particularly stable, and many carbohydrates therefore exist in an equilibrium between open-chain and cyclic forms. Glucose, for instance, exists in aqueous solution primarily in the six-membered [[term:pyranose|term-00001]] form resulting from intramolecular nucleophilic addition of the –OH group at C5 to the C1 carbonyl group ([[xref:fig-00002]]). The name [[i:pyranose]] is derived from [[i:pyran,]] the name of the unsaturated six-membered cyclic ether.

<!-- SEG:m00303:para:para-00003 -->
Like cyclohexane rings (), pyranose rings have a chairlike geometry with axial and equatorial substituents. By convention, the rings are usually drawn by placing the hemiacetal oxygen atom at the right rear, as shown in [[xref:fig-00002]]. Note that an –OH group on the right in a Fischer projection is on the bottom face of the pyranose ring, and an –OH group on the left in a Fischer projection is on the top face of the ring. For D sugars, the terminal –CH[[sub:2]]OH group is on the top of the ring, whereas for L sugars, the –CH[[sub:2]]OH group is on the bottom.

<!-- SEG:m00303:para:para-00004 -->
When an open-chain monosaccharide cyclizes to a pyranose form, a new chirality center is generated at the former carbonyl carbon and two diastereomers, called [[term:anomers|term-00002]], are produced. The hemiacetal carbon atom is referred to as the [[term:anomeric center|term-00003]]. For example, glucose cyclizes reversibly in aqueous solution to a 37 : 63 mixture of two anomers ([[xref:fig-00002]]). The compound with its newly generated –OH group at C1 cis to the –OH at the lowest chirality center in a Fischer projection is called the [[term:[[i:α]] anomer|term-00004]]; its full name is [[i:α]]-D-glucopyranose. The compound with its newly generated –OH group trans to the –OH at the lowest chirality center is called the [[term:[[i:β]] anomer|term-00005]]; its full name is [[i:β]]-D-glucopyranose. Note that in [[i:β]]-D-glucopyranose, all the substituents on the ring are equatorial. Thus, [[i:β]]-D-glucopyranose is the least sterically crowded and most stable of the eight D aldohexoses.

<!-- SEG:m00303:caption:fig-00002-caption -->
[[b:Glucose in its cyclic pyranose forms.]] As explained in the text, two anomers are formed by cyclization of glucose. The molecule whose newly formed –OH group at C1 is cis to the oxygen atom on the lowest chirality center (C5) in a Fischer projection is the [[i:α]] anomer. The molecule whose newly formed –OH group is trans to the oxygen atom on the lowest chirality center in a Fischer projection is the [[i:β]] anomer.

<!-- SEG:m00303:alt:fig-00002-alt -->
The ball-and-stick model, Fischer projection, and Haworth projection of alpha-D-glucopyranose and beta-D-glucopyranose. Formation of cyclic hemiacetal creates an additional chirality center giving two diastereomeric forms designated as alpha and beta.

<!-- SEG:m00303:para:para-00005 -->
Some monosaccharides also exist in a five-membered cyclic hemiacetal form called a [[term:furanose|term-00006]]. D-Fructose, for instance, exists in water solution as 68% [[i:β]]-pyranose, 2.7% [[i:α]]-pyranose, 0.5% open-chain, 22.4% [[i:β]]-furanose, and 6.2% [[i:α]]-furanose. The pyranose form results from addition of the –OH at C6 to the carbonyl group, while the furanose form results from addition of the –OH at C5 to the carbonyl group ([[xref:fig-00003]]).

<!-- SEG:m00303:caption:fig-00003-caption -->
[[b:Pyranose and furanose forms of fructose in aqueous solution.]] The two pyranose anomers result from addition of the C6 –OH group to the C2 carbonyl; the two furanose anomers result from addition of the C5 –OH group to the C2 carbonyl.

<!-- SEG:m00303:alt:fig-00003-alt -->
The Fischer and Haworth projection of beta-D-fructopyranose and beta-D-fructofuranose. Formation of pyranose and furanose anomers form through the hydroxyl group addition to C 2 carbonyl.

<!-- SEG:m00303:para:para-00006 -->
Both anomers of D-glucopyranose can be crystallized and purified. Pure [[i:α]]-D-glucopyranose has a melting point of 146 °C and a specific rotation [[[i:α]]][[sub:D]] = +112.2; pure [[i:β]]-D-glucopyranose has a melting point of 148 to 155 °C and a specific rotation [[[i:α]]][[sub:D]] = +18.7. When a sample of either pure anomer is dissolved in water, however, its optical rotation slowly changes until it reaches a constant value of +52.6. That is, the specific rotation of the [[i:α]]-anomer solution decreases from +112.2 to +52.6, and the specific rotation of the [[i:β]]-anomer solution increases from +18.7 to +52.6. Called [[term:mutarotation|term-00007]], this change in optical rotation is due to the slow interconversion of the pure anomers to give the same 37 : 63 equilibrium mixture.

<!-- SEG:m00303:para:para-00007 -->
Mutarotation occurs by a reversible ring-opening of each anomer to the open-chain aldehyde, followed by reclosure. Although the equilibration is slow at neutral pH, it is catalyzed by both acid and base.

<!-- SEG:m00303:alt:fig-00004-alt -->
Haworth projection of reversible ring-opening of alpha-D-glucopyranose to beta-D-glucopyranose. The hydroxyl group at C 1 is axial in alpha and equatorial in beta form.

<!-- SEG:m00303:example-title:exam-00001-title -->
Drawing the Chair Conformation of an Aldohexose

<!-- SEG:m00303:para:para-00008 -->
D-Mannose differs from D-glucose in its stereochemistry at C2. Draw D-mannose in its chairlike pyranose form.

<!-- SEG:m00303:para-title:para-00009-title -->
Strategy

<!-- SEG:m00303:para:para-00009 -->
First draw a Fischer projection of D-mannose. Then lay it on its side, and curl it around so that the –CHO group (C1) is on the right front and the –CH[[sub:2]]OH group (C6) is toward the left rear. Now, connect the –OH at C5 to the C1 carbonyl group to form the pyranose ring. In drawing the chair form, raise the leftmost carbon (C4) up and drop the rightmost carbon (C1) down.

<!-- SEG:m00303:para-title:para-00010-title -->
Solution

<!-- SEG:m00303:alt:fig-00005-alt -->
The Fischer projection of D-mannose is depicted. The pyranose ring is obtained by joining the carbonyl carbon to the hydroxyl group at C 5 forming a six-membered ring.

<!-- SEG:m00303:example-title:exam-00002-title -->
Drawing the Chair Conformation of a Pyranose

<!-- SEG:m00303:para:para-00011 -->
Draw [[i:β]]-L-glucopyranose in its more stable chair conformation.

<!-- SEG:m00303:para-title:para-00012-title -->
Strategy

<!-- SEG:m00303:para:para-00012 -->
It’s probably easiest to begin by drawing the chair conformation of [[i:β]]-D-glucopyranose. Then draw its mirror-image L enantiomer by changing the stereochemistry at every position on the ring, and carry out a ring-flip to give the more stable chair conformation. Note that the –CH[[sub:2]]OH group is on the bottom face of the ring in the L enantiomer, as is the anomeric –OH.

<!-- SEG:m00303:para-title:para-00013-title -->
Solution

<!-- SEG:m00303:alt:fig-00006-alt -->
Beta-L-Glucopyranose can be drawn by inversion followed by ring flip of beta-D-glucopyranose structure.

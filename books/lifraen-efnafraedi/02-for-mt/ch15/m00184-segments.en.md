Aromatic Ions

<!-- SEG:m00184:para:para-00001 -->
According to the Hückel criteria for aromaticity, a molecule must be cyclic, conjugated (nearly planar with a [[i:p]] orbital on each atom), and have 4[[i:n]] + 2 [[i:π]] electrons. Nothing in this definition says that the number of [[i:π]] electrons must be the same as the number of atoms in the ring or that the substance must be neutral. In fact, the numbers can differ and the substance can be an ion. Thus, both the cyclopentadienyl anion and the cycloheptatrienyl cation are aromatic even though both are ions and neither contains a six-membered ring.

<!-- SEG:m00184:alt:fig-00001-alt -->
Cyclopentadienyl anion and cycloheptatrienyl cation each have six pi electrons and are aromatic.

<!-- SEG:m00184:para:para-00002 -->
To understand why the cyclopentadienyl anion and the cycloheptatrienyl cation are aromatic, imagine starting from the related neutral hydrocarbons, 1,3-cyclopentadiene and 1,3,5-cycloheptatriene, and removing one hydrogen from the saturated CH[[sub:2]] carbon in each. If that carbon then rehybridizes from [[i:sp]][[sup:3]] to [[i:sp]][[sup:2]], the resultant products would be fully conjugated, with a [[i:p]] orbital on every carbon. There are three ways in which the hydrogen might be removed.

<!-- SEG:m00184:item:list-00001-item-1 -->
The hydrogen can be removed with [[i:both]] electrons (H[[b::]][[sup:–]]) from the C–H bond, leaving a carbocation as product.

<!-- SEG:m00184:item:list-00001-item-2 -->
The hydrogen can be removed with [[i:one]] electron (H[[b:·]]) from the C–H bond, leaving a carbon radical as product.

<!-- SEG:m00184:item:list-00001-item-3 -->
The hydrogen can be removed with [[i:no]] electrons (H[[sup:+]]) from the C–H bond, leaving a carbanion as product.

<!-- SEG:m00184:para:para-00003 -->
All the potential products formed by removing a hydrogen from 1,3-cyclopentadiene and from 1,3,5-cycloheptatriene can be drawn with numerous resonance structures, but Hückel’s rule predicts that only the six-[[i:π]]-electron cyclopentadienyl anion and cycloheptatrienyl cation can be aromatic. The other products are predicted by the 4[[i:n]] + 2 rule to be unstable and antiaromatic ([[xref:fig-00002]]).

<!-- SEG:m00184:caption:fig-00002-caption -->
[[b:The aromatic six-[[i:π]]-electron cyclopentadienyl anion and the six-[[i:π]]-electron cycloheptatrienyl cation.]] The anion can be formed by removing a hydrogen ion (H[[sup:+]]) from the CH[[sub:2]] group of 1,3-cyclopentadiene. The cation can be generated by removing a hydride ion (H[[b::]][[sup:–]]) from the CH[[sub:2]] group of 1,3,5-cycloheptatriene.

<!-- SEG:m00184:alt:fig-00002-alt -->
1,3-Cyclopentadiene yields different products if a hydrogen is removed and replaced with a negative charge, a radical, or a positive charge. The same is true in the case of 1,3,5-cycloheptatriene.

<!-- SEG:m00184:para:para-00004 -->
In practice, both the four-[[i:π]]-electron cyclopentadienyl cation and the five-[[i:π]]-electron cyclopentadienyl radical are highly reactive and difficult to prepare. Neither shows any sign of the stability expected for an aromatic system. The six-[[i:π]]-electron cyclopentadienyl anion, by contrast, is easily prepared and remarkably stable ([[xref:fig-00003]]a). In fact, the anion is so stable and easily formed that 1,3-cyclopentadiene is one of the most acidic hydrocarbons known, with p[[i:K]][[sub:a]] = 16, a value comparable to that of water!

<!-- SEG:m00184:para:para-00005 -->
In the same way, the seven-[[i:π]]-electron cycloheptatrienyl radical and eight-[[i:π]]-electron anion are reactive and difficult to prepare, while the six-[[i:π]]-electron cycloheptatrienyl cation is extraordinarily stable ([[xref:fig-00003]]b). In fact, the cycloheptatrienyl cation was first prepared more than a century ago by reaction of 1,3,5-cycloheptatriene with Br[[sub:2]], although its structure was not recognized at the time.

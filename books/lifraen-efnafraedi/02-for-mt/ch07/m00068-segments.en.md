Stability of Alkenes

<!-- SEG:m00068:para:para-00001 -->
Although the cis–trans interconversion of alkene isomers does not occur spontaneously, it can often be made to happen by treating the alkene with a strong acid catalyst. If we interconvert [[i:cis]]-2-butene with [[i:trans]]-2-butene and allow them to reach equilibrium, we find that they aren’t of equal stability. The trans isomer is more stable than the cis isomer by 2.8 kJ/mol (0.66 kcal/mol) at room temperature, corresponding to a 76&#8201;:&#8201;24 ratio.

<!-- SEG:m00068:alt:fig-00001-alt -->
A reaction shows trans-but-2-ene and cis-but-2-ene interconvert in acid catalyst to reach a 76 to 24 percent ratio.

<!-- SEG:m00068:para:para-00002 -->
Cis alkenes are less stable than their trans isomers because of steric strain between the two larger substituents on the same side of the double bond. This is the same kind of steric interference that we saw previously in the axial conformation of methylcyclohexane ().

<!-- SEG:m00068:alt:fig-00002-alt -->
The space-filling models of cis-2-butene and trans-2-butene. The gray and white spheres represent carbon and hydrogen atoms, respectively. Steric strain is shown between methyl groups in cis-2-butene.

<!-- SEG:m00068:para:para-00003 -->
Although it’s sometimes possible to find relative stabilities of alkene isomers by establishing a cis–trans equilibrium through treatment with strong acid, a more general method is to take advantage of the fact that alkenes undergo a [[i:hydrogenation]] reaction to give the corresponding alkane when treated with H[[sub:2]] gas in the presence of a catalyst such as palladium or platinum.

<!-- SEG:m00068:alt:fig-00003-alt -->
Trans-2-butene and cis-2-butene, each react with molecular hydrogen in the presence of palladium catalyst to form butane.

<!-- SEG:m00068:para:para-00004 -->
Energy diagrams for the hydrogenation reactions of [[i:cis]]- and [[i:trans]]-2-butene are shown in [[xref:fig-00004]]. Because [[i:cis]]-2-butene is less stable than [[i:trans]]-2-butene by 2.8 kJ/mol, the energy diagram shows the cis alkene at a higher energy level. After reaction, however, both curves are at the same energy level (butane). It therefore follows that Δ[[i:G]]° for reaction of the cis isomer must be larger than Δ[[i:G]]° for reaction of the trans isomer by 2.8 kJ/mol. In other words, more energy is released in the hydrogenation of the cis isomer than the trans isomer because the cis isomer has more energy to begin with.

<!-- SEG:m00068:caption:fig-00004-caption -->
[[b:Energy diagrams for hydrogenation of [[i:cis]]- and [[i:trans]]-2-butene]]. The cis isomer is higher in energy than the trans isomer by about 2.8 kJ/mol and therefore releases more energy when hydrogenated.

<!-- SEG:m00068:alt:fig-00004-alt -->
An energy diagram for the formation of butane from cis- and trans-2-butene. Cis is higher in energy than trans, and both are higher in energy than butane.

<!-- SEG:m00068:para:para-00005 -->
If we were to measure the so-called heats of hydrogenation (Δ[[i:H]]°[[sub:hydrog]]) for two double-bond isomers and find their difference, we could determine the relative stabilities of cis and trans isomers without having to measure an equilibrium position. [[i:cis]]-2-Butene, for instance, has &#916;[[i:H]]&#176;[[sub:hydrog]] = &#8722;119 kJ/mol(&#8722;28.3 kcal/mol), while [[i:trans]]-2-butene has &#916;[[i:H]]&#176;[[sub:hydrog]] = &#8722;115 kJ/mol(&#8722;27.4 kcal/mol)—a difference of 4 kJ/mol.

<!-- SEG:m00068:alt:fig-00005-alt -->
The structures of cis- and trans-2-butene with delta H naught hydrogenation equals minus 120 and minus 116 kilojoules per mole, respectively.

<!-- SEG:m00068:para:para-00006 -->
The 4 kJ/mol energy difference between the 2-butene isomers calculated from heats of hydrogenation agrees reasonably well with the 2.8 kJ/mol energy difference calculated from equilibrium data, but the values aren’t exactly the same for two reasons. First, there is probably some experimental error, because heats of hydrogenation are difficult to measure accurately. Second, heats of reaction and equilibrium constants don’t measure exactly the same thing. Heats of reaction measure enthalpy changes, Δ[[i:H]]°, whereas equilibrium constants measure free-energy changes, Δ[[i:G]]°, so we might expect a slight difference between the two.

<!-- SEG:m00068:para:para-00008 -->
The stability order of substituted alkenes is due to a combination of two factors. One is a stabilizing interaction between the [[MATH:8]] [[i:π]] orbital and adjacent C−H [[i:σ]] bonds on substituents. In valence-bond language, the interaction is called [[term:hyperconjugation|term-00001]]. In a molecular orbital description, there is a bonding MO that extends over the four-atom [[MATH:9]] grouping, as shown in [[xref:fig-00007]]. The more substituents present on the double bond, the more hyperconjugation occurs and the more stable the alkene.

<!-- SEG:m00068:caption:fig-00007-caption -->
[[b:Hyperconjugation is a stabilizing interaction between the [[MATH:10]] [[i:π]] orbital and adjacent C−H [[i:σ]] bonds on substituents]]. The more substituents there are, the greater the stabilization of the alkene.

<!-- SEG:m00068:para:para-00009 -->
A second factor that contributes to alkene stability involves bond strengths. A bond between a [[i:sp]][[sup:2]] carbon and a [[i:sp]][[sup:3]] carbon is somewhat stronger than a bond between two [[i:sp]][[sup:3]] carbons. Thus, in comparing 1-butene and 2-butene, the monosubstituted isomer has one [[i:sp]][[sup:3]]–[[i:sp]][[sup:3]] bond and one [[i:sp]][[sup:3]]–[[i:sp]][[sup:2]] bond, while the disubstituted isomer has two [[i:sp]][[sup:3]]–[[i:sp]][[sup:2]] bonds. More highly substituted alkenes always have a higher ratio of [[i:sp]][[sup:3]]–[[i:sp]][[sup:2]] bonds to [[i:sp]][[sup:3]]–[[i:sp]][[sup:3]] bonds than less highly substituted alkenes and are therefore more stable.

Structures of of 2-butene (more stable due to more s p 2 to s p 3 bonds) and 1-butene (less stable).

Spectroscopy of Aldehydes and Ketones

<!-- SEG:m00225:title:sect-00001-title -->
Infrared Spectroscopy

<!-- SEG:m00225:para:para-00001 -->
Aldehydes and ketones show a strong [[MATH:1]] bond absorption in the IR region from 1660 to 1770 cm[[sup:–1]], as the spectra of benzaldehyde and cyclohexanone demonstrate ([[xref:fig-00001]]). In addition, aldehydes show two characteristic C–H absorptions between 2700–2760 and 2800–2860 cm[[sup:–1]]. These absorbances are important for distinguishing between aldehydes and ketones. The higher-frequency absorbance is sometimes obscured in cases where the compound has numerous saturated C–H groups, but the lower-frequency peak is almost always visible.

<!-- SEG:m00225:caption:fig-00001-caption -->
[[b:Infrared spectra of (a) benzaldehyde and (b) cyclohexanone.]]

<!-- SEG:m00225:alt:fig-00001-alt -->
I R spectra of benzaldehyde with carbonyl peak at 1700 and aldehyde hydrogen peak around 2800, and of cyclohexanone with carbonyl peak around 1700 wavenumbers.

<!-- SEG:m00225:para:para-00002 -->
The exact position of the [[MATH:2]] absorption is diagnostic of the nature of the carbonyl group. As the data in [[xref:table-00001]] indicate, saturated aldehydes usually show carbonyl absorptions near 1730 cm[[sup:–1]] in the IR spectrum, but conjugation of the aldehyde to an aromatic ring or a double bond lowers the absorption by 25 cm[[sup:–1]] to near 1705 cm[[sup:–1]]. Saturated aliphatic ketones and cyclohexanones both absorb near 1715 cm[[sup:–1]], and conjugation with a double bond or an aromatic ring again lowers the absorption by 30 cm[[sup:–1]] to 1685–1690 cm[[sup:–1]]. Angle strain in the carbonyl group, caused by reducing the ring size of cyclic ketones to four or five, raises the absorption position. Cyclohexanone has its [[MATH:3]] stretch absorbance at 1715 cm[[sup:–1]] while cyclopentanone’s carbonyl stretch is at 1750 cm[[sup:–1]] and cyclobutanone’s is at 1785 cm[[sup:–1]].

<!-- SEG:m00225:para:para-00003 -->
The values given in [[xref:table-00001]] are remarkably constant from one aldehyde or ketone to another. As a result, IR spectroscopy is a powerful tool for identifying the kind of a carbonyl group in a molecule of unknown structure. An unknown that shows an IR absorption at 1730 cm[[sup:–1]] is almost certainly an aldehyde rather than a ketone; an unknown that shows an IR absorption at 1750 cm[[sup:–1]] is almost certainly a cyclopentanone, and so on.

Nuclear Magnetic Resonance Spectroscopy

<!-- SEG:m00225:para:para-00006 -->
Aldehyde protons (RC[[b:H]]O) absorb near 10 [[i:δ]] in the [[sup:1]]H NMR spectrum and are very distinctive because no other absorptions occur in this region. The aldehyde proton shows spin–spin coupling with protons on the neighboring carbon, with coupling constant [[i:J]] ≈ 3 Hz. Acetaldehyde, for example, shows a quartet at 9.79 [[i:δ]] for the aldehyde proton, indicating that there are three protons neighboring the –CHO group ([[xref:fig-00002]]).

<!-- SEG:m00225:caption:fig-00002-caption -->
[[b:[[sup:1]]H NMR spectrum of acetaldehyde.]] The absorption of the aldehyde proton appears at 9.79 [[i:δ]] and is split into a quartet.

<!-- SEG:m00225:alt:fig-00002-alt -->
1 H N M R spectrum of acetaldehyde with shifts at 2.23 (doublet) and 9.79 (quartet). Relative areas are 3 and 1 respectively.

<!-- SEG:m00225:para:para-00007 -->
Hydrogens on the carbon next to a carbonyl group are slightly deshielded and usually absorb near 2.0 to 2.3 [[i:δ]]. The acetaldehyde methyl group in [[xref:fig-00002]], for instance, absorbs at 2.20 [[i:δ]]. Methyl ketones are particularly distinctive because they always show a sharp three-proton singlet near 2.1 [[i:δ]].

<!-- SEG:m00225:para:para-00008 -->
The carbonyl-group carbon atoms of aldehydes and ketones have characteristic [[sup:13]]C NMR resonances in the range 190 to 215 [[i:δ]]. Since no other kinds of carbons absorb in this range, the presence of an NMR absorption near 200 [[i:δ]] is clear evidence for a carbonyl group. Saturated aldehyde or ketone carbons usually absorb in the region from 200 to 215 [[i:δ]], while aromatic and [[i:α]],[[i:β]]-unsaturated carbonyl carbons absorb in the 190 to 200 [[i:δ]] region.

<!-- SEG:m00225:alt:fig-00003-alt -->

<!-- SEG:m00225:title:sect-00004-title -->
Mass Spectrometry

<!-- SEG:m00225:para:para-00009 -->
As discussed in , aliphatic aldehydes and ketones that have hydrogens on their gamma ([[i:γ]]) carbon atoms undergo a characteristic mass spectral cleavage called the [[term:McLafferty rearrangement|term-00001]]. A hydrogen atom is transferred from the [[i:γ]] carbon to the carbonyl oxygen, the bond between the [[i:α]] and [[i:β]] carbons is broken, and a neutral alkene fragment is produced. The charge remains with the oxygen-containing fragment.

<!-- SEG:m00225:alt:fig-00004-alt -->
An aliphatic aldehyde or ketone undergoes Mclafferty rearrangement to form an alkene and a fragment containing oxygen, with bond migration occurring around a six-memberd ringlike framework.

<!-- SEG:m00225:para:para-00010 -->
In addition to fragmentation by the McLafferty rearrangement, aldehydes and ketones also undergo cleavage of the bond between the carbonyl group and the [[i:α]] carbon, called an [[i:α cleavage]]. Alpha cleavage yields a neutral radical and a resonance-stabilized acyl cation.

<!-- SEG:m00225:alt:fig-00005-alt -->
An aldehyde or ketone undergoes alpha cleavage to produce a neutral radical R and a resonance-stabilized acyl cation (R prime single bond to C positive double bond to O).

<!-- SEG:m00225:para:para-00011 -->
Fragment ions from both McLafferty rearrangement and [[i:α]] cleavage are visible in the mass spectrum of 5-methyl-2-hexanone shown in [[xref:fig-00006]]. McLafferty rearrangement with loss of 2-methylpropene yields a fragment with [[i:m/z]] = 58. Alpha cleavage occurs primarily at the more substituted side of the carbonyl group, leading to a [CH[[sub:3]]CO][[sup:+]] fragment with [[i:m/z]] = 43.

<!-- SEG:m00225:caption:fig-00006-caption -->
[[b:Mass spectrum and the related reactions of 5-methyl-2-hexanone.]] The peak at [[i:m]]/[[i:z]] = 58 is due to McLafferty rearrangement. The abundant peak at [[i:m]]/[[i:z]] = 43 is due to [[i:α]] cleavage at the more highly substituted side of the carbonyl group. Note that the peak due to the molecular ion is very small.

<!-- SEG:m00225:title:sect-00006-title -->
Enantioselective Synthesis

<!-- SEG:m00225:para:para-00014 -->
Whenever a chiral product is formed by reaction between achiral reagents, the product is racemic; that is, both enantiomers of the product are formed in equal amounts. The epoxidation reaction of geraniol with [[i:m]]-chloroperoxybenzoic acid, for instance, gives a racemic mixture of (2[[i:S]],3[[i:S]]) and (2[[i:R]],3[[i:R]]) epoxides.

<!-- SEG:m00225:alt:fig-00007-alt -->
Geraniol reacts with m-chloroperoxybenzoic acid to form 50 percent of (2 S, 3 S) and (2 R, 3 R) epoxides.

<!-- SEG:m00225:para:para-00015 -->
Unfortunately, it’s usually the case that only one enantiomer of a given drug or other important substance has the desired biological properties. The other enantiomer might be inactive or even dangerous. Thus, much work is currently being done on developing [[i:enantioselective]] methods of synthesis, which yield only one of the two possible enantiomers. So important has enantioselective synthesis become that the 2001 Nobel Prize in Chemistry was awarded to three pioneers in the field: William S. Knowles of the Monsanto Co., K. Barry Sharpless of the Scripps Research Institute, and Ryoji Noyori of Nagoya University.

<!-- SEG:m00225:para:para-00016 -->
Several approaches to enantioselective synthesis have been developed, but the most efficient are those that use chiral catalysts to temporarily hold a substrate molecule in an unsymmetrical environment—the same strategy that nature uses when catalyzing reactions with chiral enzymes. While in that unsymmetrical environment, the substrate may be more open to reaction on one side than on another, leading to an excess of one enantiomeric product over another. As an analogy, think about picking up a coffee mug in your right hand to take a drink. The mug by itself is achiral, but as soon as you pick it up by the handle, it becomes chiral. One side of the mug now faces toward you so you can drink from it, but the other side faces away. The two sides are different, with one side much more accessible to you than the other.

<!-- SEG:m00225:caption:fig-00008-caption -->
[[b:A substance made from the tartaric acid found at the bottom of this wine vat catalyzes enantioselective reactions.]] (credit: “Pinot Noir fermentation in open vat” by Pipers Brook Vineyard Media/Flickr, CC BY 2.0)

<!-- SEG:m00225:alt:fig-00008-alt -->
A photo of a purplish-brown foamy, brownie-like substance.

<!-- SEG:m00225:para:para-00017 -->
Among the thousands of enantioselective reactions now known, one of the most useful is the so-called Sharpless epoxidation, in which an allylic alcohol, such as geraniol, is treated with [[i:tert]]-butyl hydroperoxide, (CH[[sub:3]])[[sub:3]]C&#x2013;OOH, in the presence of titanium tetraisopropoxide and diethyl tartrate (DET) as a chiral auxiliary reagent. When ([[i:R,R]]) tartrate is used, geraniol is converted into its 2[[i:S]],3[[i:S]] epoxide with 98% selectivity, whereas the use of ([[i:S,S]]) tartrate gives the 2[[i:R]],3[[i:R]] epoxide enantiomer. We say that the major product in each case is formed with an [[i:enantiomeric excess]] of 96%, meaning that 4% of the product is racemic (2% 2[[i:S]],3[[i:S]] plus 2% 2[[i:R]],3[[i:R]]) and an extra 96% of a single enantiomer is formed. The mechanistic details by which the chiral catalyst works are a bit complex, although it appears that a chiral complex of two tartrate molecules with one titanium is involved.

<!-- SEG:m00225:title:sect-00008-title -->
Summary

<!-- SEG:m00225:para:para-00018 -->
Aldehydes and ketones are among the most important of all functional groups, both in the chemical industry and in biological pathways. In this chapter, we’ve looked at some of their typical reactions. Aldehydes are normally prepared in the laboratory by oxidation of primary alcohols or by partial reduction of esters. Ketones are similarly prepared by oxidation of secondary alcohols.

<!-- SEG:m00225:para:para-00019 -->
The [[b:nucleophilic addition reaction]] is the most common general reaction type for aldehydes and ketones. Many different kinds of products can be prepared by nucleophilic additions. Aldehydes and ketones are reduced by NaBH[[sub:4]] or LiAlH[[sub:4]] to yield primary and secondary alcohols, respectively. Addition of Grignard reagents to aldehydes and ketones also gives alcohols (secondary and tertiary, respectively), and addition of HCN yields [[b:cyanohydrins]]. Primary amines add to carbonyl compounds yielding [[b:imines]], or [[b:Schiff bases]], and secondary amines yield [[b:enamines]]. Reaction of an aldehyde or ketone with hydrazine and base gives an alkane (the [[b:Wolff–Kishner reaction]]). Alcohols add to carbonyl groups to yield [[b:acetals]], which are valuable as protecting groups. Phosphorus [[b:ylides]] add to aldehydes and ketones in the [[b:Wittig reaction]] to give alkenes.

<!-- SEG:m00225:para:para-00020 -->
[[i:α]],[[i:β]]-Unsaturated aldehydes and ketones often react with nucleophiles to give the product of [[b:conjugate addition]], or [[b:1,4-addition]]. Particularly useful are the conjugate addition of an amine and the conjugate addition of an organic group by reaction with a diorganocopper reagent.

<!-- SEG:m00225:para:para-00021 -->
IR spectroscopy is helpful for identifying aldehydes and ketones. Carbonyl groups absorb in the IR range 1660 to 1770 cm[[sup:–1]], with the exact position highly diagnostic of the kind of carbonyl group present in the molecule. [[sup:13]]C NMR spectroscopy is also useful for aldehydes and ketones because their carbonyl carbons show resonances in the 190 to 215 [[i:δ]] range. [[sup:1]]H NMR is useful for aldehyde –CHO protons, which absorb near 10 [[i:δ]]. Aldehydes and ketones undergo two characteristic kinds of fragmentation in the mass spectrometer: [[i:α]] cleavage and McLafferty rearrangement.
